Research Value

Why a collective
dataset matters

01
The sample size problem

The effect size documented by PEAR — approximately 0.0003 — requires roughly 26,000 independent measurement events to reach p < 0.05 at adequate power. No individual experimenter will accumulate that in a reasonable time frame. A collective dataset crosses that threshold orders of magnitude faster.

02
Cross-operator analysis

The most scientifically interesting question is not whether any individual shows an effect — it is whether the effect replicates across independent operators under similar conditions. Collective data allows the kind of operator-level comparison the original PEAR studies used to validate their findings.

03
Protocol comparison

Live quantum, buffered quantum, and local entropy test structurally different hypotheses. A collective dataset with enough sessions in each condition makes it possible to compare effect sizes across protocols — something no single experimenter's dataset can do with statistical confidence.

Cumulative Z-Score Drift

Drift from zero across sessions in chronological order. Sustained positive drift suggests a consistent above-chance effect. A flat walk is the null result.

Z-Score Distribution

How often each z-score range occurred. A normal distribution centred at zero is the null hypothesis. Positive skew suggests a systematic effect.

Phase Decline — Early vs Late Z per Session

Early-phase z-score (orange) and late-phase z-score (purple) plotted per session in chronological order. When the early line consistently sits above the late line, the PEAR decline effect is present — the effect appears stronger at the start of sessions and diminishes as the session continues.
